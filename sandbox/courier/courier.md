Courier ops is a dispatch shop that already has packages in the warehouse and needs a dispatcher to plan who goes where. The product is not a customer booking site. Clerks on the intake side register incoming parcels — label, size, recipient address — or import a warehouse list. When a package is registered, its address is placed on a stop. If that address already exists as a stop, the existing stop is reused. Dispatchers never plant stops on a map by hand. A stop is just a delivery address that already belongs to exactly one zone. An address never sits in two zones.

The city is carved into delivery zones. Each zone has a name and a geographic boundary. Stops that fall inside the boundary belong to that zone. Dispatchers can later draw or import those boundaries and preview coverage, but the planning idea does not wait on a fancy map editor. What matters first is that zones exist, stops already live in them because of intake, and a route is a plan that walks through zones in order.

A delivery route starts empty. The dispatcher creates one, then enters zones onto it in sequence — North, then East, then West, and so on. The moment a zone is entered, every stop that already lives in that zone appears on the route. Stops in zones the route has not entered stay off the route. If the dispatcher drops a zone, those stops leave with it and the driving path is figured again. The dispatcher can also reorder the zone list when a different sequence is a shorter drive.

The path is not a free-form street-by-street tour the dispatcher draws. It is calculated from the ordered list of zones, then checked for a shorter driver path. Neighboring zones sometimes make the naive order wasteful — East then North then West might be longer than North then West then East because North sits against West. Recalculating can swap adjacent zones when that shortens the trip. The zone order on the route is allowed to change when the shorter path says so. Until at least one zone is entered there is no path at all.

Packages hang on stops that are already on the route. The dispatcher assigns a parcel to a stop, takes it off, or moves it to another on-route stop. A package cannot hang on a stop the route does not visit. Listing packages on a route shows only what is hung for that plan. Searching packages by label is a convenience when the list gets long. Searching zones and previewing which stops a zone would contribute help the dispatcher pick the next zone before adding it.

The dispatcher’s day lives in two lists: routes and zones. From home they open today’s routes or browse zones. Creating or opening a route lands them in a planner: add a zone (often a small picker), reorder, recalculate, remove, then open a stop to hang or move packages. Zone detail is for looking at a boundary and later editing it. Fleet and driver screens exist in the product imagination but they are not required to prove planning.

There are other themes around this shop that are real and not yet the point of the first proof. Registering packages and importing warehouse lists belong to intake. Staffing a route with a driver and a vehicle, then dispatching or recalling it, belongs to fleet. The driver app that executes the route is a sibling surface, not this slice. Live oversee of a path change can be published later. The first thing to prove is that a dispatcher can create a route, enter zones so stops appear, let the path come from zone order plus a shorter-path check, and hang a package on a stop that is actually on that route.

Language that must stay consistent: a route holds an ordered zone sequence and a calculated path, and it collects package-on-stop assignments. A zone owns a boundary and the stops inside it. A stop records one address and belongs to one zone. A package carries a recipient address and resolves to a zone stop when it is registered. Packages may only hang on stops that are on the route. Build the planning core as zones first, then packages, then routes that depend on both.
